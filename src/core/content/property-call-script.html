This is a TWO CALL process, and you must know which call you are on. CALL ONE is discovery. First time this branch has heard from us: you get the facts, what has sold done up on the street, what has been rejected, THEIR figure if they will give one, the video and the measurements, the email address, and a booked time to ring back. You never say a number of our own on call one. Not once, not roughly, not "just to give you an idea". We do the homework first, and that is exactly what you tell them.
CALL TWO is the offer call, stage 6 below. You only make it after the homework is done and the money boxes on your screen are filled with the director's confirmed figures. That is where the ballpark question lives, and a figure out of their mouth is the prize on both calls. What you never do on either call: a formal or binding offer, or a viewing. You never view a property: our builder does that, and he prices the work while he is there. Read the blue lines aloud. Grey lines are notes for you only. 3. The discovery questions
This is the whole job of call one, so take your time. There is no money conversation coming to eat the call any more, and every answer here is a pound note: the homework and the offer are built from what you write down. Conversational, not a form. Acknowledge each answer, "ah okay", "that makes sense", before the next question. Start with the three that matter most.
YOU: "Is it vacant, or is there a tenant in?"
If tenanted: are they staying or leaving, and what rent are they paying? An empty house costs the vendor money every month, which is why this question is first.
YOU: "And what sort of condition is it in, ready to move into or does it need work?"
"It needs a bit of work" is not an answer, so never write it down and move on. That same sentence covers a five grand tidy-up and a forty grand strip-out, and the difference between those two IS our offer. So when they say it needs work, slow down and have a proper conversation about it. Three or four questions, one at a time, acknowledging each answer.
YOU: "Ah okay. When you say it needs work, what sort of thing are we talking? Is it more cosmetic, kitchen, bathroom, carpets, decorating, or is it a proper full refurb?"
Make them choose between the two, because they are two completely different prices. If they hedge, put it another way: "Could someone move in and live in it while the work's being done, or is it a shell?"
YOU: "And how are the big four, the roof, any damp, the electrics and the boiler? Have any of them been done?"
This is the money question of the four. A rewire, a new boiler, a roof or a damp course are thousands each, and none of them show up in photographs. Ask them one at a time and take the answer for each. "When was the boiler last done, do you know?" tells you more than "is it fine?".
YOU: "And is it dry? Any leaks, anything coming in, any staining on the ceilings? What's the roof like, has it been done or is it the original?"
ASK THIS ON EVERY SINGLE HOUSE, even one described as immaculate. Water is the one problem that turns a fifteen grand refurb into a forty grand one, and it is the first thing our builder looks for. A photograph never shows it, and half the time the agent will tell you straight out if you just ask.

Never take "I think it's fine". Push once, lightly: "has anyone been up on it?" and "has there ever been a leak in there, even one that's been sorted?" A fixed leak still leaves a ceiling, a floor and sometimes a wall behind it.

A leak is not a reason to walk away, it is the reason the price comes down. Do not react to it, do not sound put off, and never say what it will cost us. Write their exact words in the Houses tab. "Damp in the back bedroom, roof done about ten years ago" is worth more to the director than any other line on the card.
YOU: "How old are the kitchen and the bathroom? And are the windows double glazed?"
Original 1980s kitchen and bathroom means both are coming out. Single glazing is a whole-house job.
YOU: "Has anybody been round and priced the work up? What sort of number did they come back with?"
Agents hear what other buyers' builders said. A number from that is free homework, and it is a number they gave you, which is useful again on call two.
Then write it in their words in the Houses tab, not as "needs work". "Kitchen and bathroom original, boiler 15 years, damp in the back bedroom, roof fine" is what the director prices from. A one-word note is a call we have to make twice.

And when they come back with a bigger number, laugh and refuse to go all the way: "I'm not going all the way up there." That is what pulls the real figure out. Said flat and serious it sounds like a demand and they close up.
How to climb, one rung at a time.
Rung 0. You are at [offer_open]. Do not move at all until they have given you something: a figure, a reason, a bit of the vendor's position. A moan is not a reason.
Rung 1. They give you something. Now move ONE step up the ladder, and make them see it cost you. Say: "Look, if it's vacant and they want it gone, I could probably stretch to [the next rung on the ladder]. Would that get it over the line?" Then stop talking.
Rung 2. Still no. Push back once, and only once, using a comp: "The thing is, the one that sold on the same street went for less than that and it didn't need the work this one needs. What would they actually take?" Then stop talking.
Rung 3. If they name a number that is on or under the last rung of the ladder, you are done negotiating. Say: "Right, that's not miles off. Let me put that exact figure to Hugo and I'll come back to you."
Rung 4. If their number is above the red box, you are finished for today. Do not creep up to it, do not hint at it, do not say "we could maybe do a bit more". Say: "That's a fair bit above where we are, so I won't mess you about. Leave it with me, I'll speak to Hugo. If it's still around in a few weeks, is it alright if I give you a ring?"
Never bid against yourself. If you say a number and they say nothing, you do NOT improve it. You wait.
When to play the director card. "Let me speak to Hugo and come back to you" is a lever, and levers only work when you pull them at the right moment. Use it when: (a) you have got a real figure out of them and you want to bank it without accepting it, (b) they ask for something formal or in writing, or (c) they push you for a commitment you are not allowed to give. Do not use it at the start of the call, and do not use it to dodge the money conversation. It buys you a reason to ring back, which is the whole point.

THEY NAME A FIGURE. This is the call. obj
YOU: "Right, that's not miles off. Let me put that exact figure to Hugo and I'll come back to you. What's a realistic time for me to ring you back, tomorrow or later in the week?"
Read this one twice before your first call. A number out of their mouth is the entire reason you rang, and on day one it happened once, at Alan Cooper Estates. The negotiator went away, asked a colleague, came back and said the vendor was "looking around the 140 mark" and why. The reply was "thank you for your time, have a great day", and the best lead of the week was gone.
Their number is not a rejection. It is the deal. It does not matter if it is miles above your ceiling. Write it in the Houses tab word for word, press Figure obtained, agree a time to ring back, and let the director decide. Never thank them and hang up on a number.

Your price, my terms. Or my price, your terms. obj
YOU: "I get it, everyone wants the best price and the quickest sale, but you can't really have both. It's either your price on my terms, or my price on your terms. If they want it gone quickly and cleanly, we're the ones who can do that, and that's what the figure reflects."
The single best line in the whole course, and it works on any money objection. You are not defending a low number, you are naming the trade they are actually making. Then give them the thing they are buying with the discount: "one viewing, no chain, no mortgage, no time wasters traipsing through every Saturday."

Corporate branches put every buyer in front of their in-house adviser before an offer goes forward, because they earn on the mortgage. Cash takes you straight out of it. Say it once and move on.
They try to talk you OUT of the property obj
YOU: "I appreciate that, and thanks for being straight with me. I'd still like to take a proper look at the numbers. Can you send me the EPC and the floor plan?"
It happens: "there's nothing in it for you", "honestly, don't bother". Sometimes it is kindness, sometimes the deal is already promised to somebody. Do not argue. If they keep blocking, ask for somebody else: "is there anyone else there who deals with the sales side?" There always is, and a branch that will not take a cash buyer seriously is a branch to note and ring back when a different person answers.
"It's priced right, that's what it's worth" obj

An agent often wins the instruction by promising a number nobody can get, and then spends months walking the vendor back down. That gap is your whole opportunity, and this question finds it without calling anybody wrong.
YOU: "The ones that actually sold on those streets went for less, and they didn't need the work this one needs. Would you say the market's moved at all since it went on?"
Never tell them they are wrong. Show them the sold prices and let them arrive at it. People like to feel clever, and an agent who reaches the conclusion himself will carry it to the vendor for you.

This is the wall that beat you four times out of four on day one, and the answer is not to argue about viewing. It is that somebody DOES view it, and that somebody is the builder, because he has to price the refurb anyway. Two birds, one trip. Say "subject to our builder", never "subject to survey", which sounds like a mortgage buyer and is not what we do.
YOU: "The only reason I'm asking first is I don't want to drag you and the vendor through all that if we're a million miles apart on price. Could you sound them out on the figure, then we'll get someone in?"
If they push again. You are not refusing to view, you are asking for an indication first, which is a reasonable thing to want. Never book the viewing yourself. Ask for the video every time: most branches have one already, and it is what lets the builder quote without anybody driving anywhere.

You are not authorised to make a formal offer, so never imply you are, never say "I'm offering", and never agree a price as though it is binding. This is the moment the director card is meant for: pull it here.

Do not invent a number of weeks. "We're cash, no chain" is the answer. A promised completion date you cannot keep is the one thing that would burn this branch permanently.

Short and confident. This is your best card, so play it whenever it is true, and it is true.

How call two ends. Whatever they said to the number, you close the same way: ask them to put it to the vendor, and book the ring-back. If they insist on a viewing before anything moves, that is what the builder's visit is for, and it only gets booked once the ballpark is agreed.
