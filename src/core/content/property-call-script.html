Ringing the agent about [property_address]

You are a real person, at a real company, ringing about a real house. You work with the director, who is a cash buyer. Your job is to find out about the property and to work the money yourself: float a figure, get them to name one back, push once. What you never do is make a formal or binding offer, and you never book a viewing. Read the blue lines aloud. Grey lines are notes for you only. One question at a time, and react to what they say before asking the next thing. If they push back, open the amber button on that line.
1. Is it still available
YOU: "Hi, hello. I'm calling about the property on [property_street], the [bedrooms] bed [property_type]. Is that one still available?"
Then stop. Nothing else. This one question tells you whether there is a call to have at all, and asking it plainly makes you sound like a buyer rather than a salesman.

2. Ask for the two minutes
YOU: "Oh lovely. I'm Pedro by the way, who am I speaking to?"
Get their name in the first thirty seconds and then use it. Write it in the Houses tab. Next time you ring this branch you ask for them by name, and that is how a cold call turns into a relationship. Say your own name first, always, or the next thing you ask sounds like a survey.
YOU: "Nice one. So, I work with our director Hugo at Unico, we buy in the area, cash. Mind if I ask you a couple of quick questions about it?"
That is the whole introduction and it does three jobs at once: a name, a company, and the word cash. Cash is the reason they will give you two minutes, so do not bury it. Then wait for the yes. From here on you say we, not I, because you are one of two people buying, not a lad with a spreadsheet.
What changed, read this once. You are not just collecting answers for somebody else to act on. You run the money conversation on this call. "I'll speak to my director and come back to you" is a tool you use later, at the moment you need it, not an opener that lets you off the hook before you have started. See stage 4.

3. The checklist
Conversational, not a form. Acknowledge each answer, "ah okay", "that makes sense", "lovely", before the next question. If they volunteer something, do not ask it again. Tick them off in the Houses tab on the right as you go. Everything you get here is ammunition for stage 4, so listen for the words empty, probate, relocating, fell through and needs work.
YOU: "Is it vacant, or is there a tenant in?"
If tenanted: are they staying or leaving, and what rent are they paying? An empty house costs the vendor money every month, which is why this question is first.
YOU: "And what sort of condition is it in, ready to move into or does it need work?"
Then the big ones: roof, damp, electrics, boiler. This is what moves the number.
YOU: "Has it had much interest? Any offers so far?"
And: has a sale ever fallen through on it? A fall-through is the single best sign a vendor will take less.
YOU: "Do you know why they're selling?"
Then: are they in a hurry, and is there an onward chain? Motivation is worth more than any other answer on this page.
YOU: "How long's it been on with you? And has the price come down at all?"
A property that has been reduced twice and is still sitting is a property the agent is being judged on. That makes them want you to be the answer, which is worth more than any argument you could make.
YOU: "Is it freehold or leasehold?"
Their answer decides which column below you use. Ask the wrong set and you sound like you have never bought a house.

If it is a flat or maisonette

  • Years left on the lease
  • Service charge per year
  • Ground rent
  • Any big one-off bills or major works planned for the block
  • Taller block? Any cladding or EWS1 issues

If it is a house or bungalow

  • Confirm it is freehold
  • Structural: roof, damp, subsidence
  • Any extensions or conversions done, and were they signed off

Never

  • Never ask a house about service charges or a lease, unless they say it is leasehold
  • Never ask a flat about subsidence, unless they raise it

4. The money
This is the part that pays for the call, and you run it. Not the director, not later, now. The whole method is one thing: you float a number without formally offering it, then you go quiet and let them tell you where the vendor really is.

[offer_open]The first figure you say, and the ONLY figure you say at first. One number. Never a range, never "somewhere in the region of X to Y".
Then climb
[ladder]Your rungs, in order. You move UP one rung at a time and only in exchange for something. Never jump two.
Walk away at
[offer_ceiling]The last rung. Past this the deal stops working, so you stop. Say the director will come back to them and end it warmly.Never say this number out loud
Worth today
[property_worth]What the sold prices nearby say it is worth as it stands. Asking is [asking_price]. This is the number your figure is built on, not the asking price.
The four rules of this section. Break none of them.
1. Say one number. A range hands them the top of it for free and you will never see the bottom again.
2. Never say the walk-away figure out loud, and never confirm it if they guess it. It is in the red box for you only.
3. After you say the number, say nothing. Count to five in your head. Whoever speaks first loses this bit.
4. Get them to say a figure. Any number out of their mouth is worth more than any number out of yours.
YOU: "Right, so I've had a proper look at this one. I've been through what's sold on the same streets and worked out roughly what it'd cost us to put it right. I'll be honest with you, I can't get near what you're asking, and I don't want to waste your time or embarrass anyone with a silly offer. But if we were to offer around [offer_open], am I in the ballpark, or am I a million miles off?"
This is the offer without offering, and it is the most important line on the page. Notice the words: "if we were to offer", not "I'd like to offer". You have said the number out loud without putting an offer on the table, so they cannot be insulted by it and you cannot be held to it. A straight lowball offer gets you a polite no and a branch that stops taking your calls. This gets you an answer.
Then be quiet. The silence after a number is the most useful thing you own. Let them fill it. Whatever they say next, write it down word for word in the Houses tab.
If they push back, this is your evidence. One comp at a time, said casually, never read out as a list: [comp_evidence]
YOU: "I only say that because a similar one nearby went for less not long ago. So what sort of figure do you think would actually get it done?"
Asking them for the number is the whole trick, and it is the second thing you must get off this call after the facts. Any figure they say is worth more than any figure you say. Write it down in the Houses tab exactly as they said it, then shut up again.
How to climb, one rung at a time.
Rung 0. You are at [offer_open]. Do not move at all until they have given you something: a figure, a reason, a bit of the vendor's position. A moan is not a reason.
Rung 1. They give you something. Now move ONE step up the ladder, and make them see it cost you. Say: "Look, if it's vacant and they want it gone, I could probably stretch to [the next rung on the ladder]. Would that get it over the line?" Then stop talking.
Rung 2. Still no. Push back once, and only once, using a comp: "The thing is, the one that sold on the same street went for less than that and it didn't need the work this one needs. What would they actually take?" Then stop talking.
Rung 3. If they name a number that is on or under the last rung of the ladder, you are done negotiating. Say: "Right, that's not miles off. Let me put that exact figure to Hugo and I'll come back to you."
Rung 4. If their number is above the red box, you are finished for today. Do not creep up to it, do not hint at it, do not say "we could maybe do a bit more". Say: "That's a fair bit above where we are, so I won't mess you about. Leave it with me, I'll speak to Hugo. If it's still around in a few weeks, is it alright if I give you a ring?"
Never bid against yourself. If you say a number and they say nothing, you do NOT improve it. You wait.
When to play the director card. "Let me speak to Hugo and come back to you" is a lever, and levers only work when you pull them at the right moment. Use it when: (a) you have got a real figure out of them and you want to bank it without accepting it, (b) they ask for something formal or in writing, or (c) they push you for a commitment you are not allowed to give. Do not use it at the start of the call, and do not use it to dodge the money conversation. It buys you a reason to ring back, which is the whole point.

"We've had higher offers than that" obj
YOU: "Okay, no worries. Are those still on the table, or did they come and go? Because it's still on the market, so I'm guessing something didn't stick."
Said lightly, never as a gotcha. Half the time the higher offer was weeks ago, or was a buyer with a house to sell. A cash buyer with no chain beats a bigger number that cannot complete, and the agent knows that better than you do.
"You'll have to view it before we put anything forward" obj

This is the most common wall on the call and it is not a no. You are not refusing to view, you are asking for an indication first, which is a reasonable thing to want. Never book the viewing yourself. Note in the Houses tab that a viewing is the next step and let the director arrange it.

You are not authorised to make a formal offer, so never imply you are, never say "I'm offering", and never agree a price as though it is binding. This is the moment the director card is meant for: pull it here.

Do not invent a number of weeks. "We're cash, no chain" is the answer. A promised completion date you cannot keep is the one thing that would burn this branch permanently.

Short and confident. This is your best card, so play it whenever it is true, and it is true.

5. Wrap up
YOU: "And what do viewings look like, weekdays, weekends, how much notice?"
Ask, but do not book one. The director arranges viewings himself. Offering to book is the fastest way to be asked a question you cannot answer.
YOU: "Right, I'll get that figure over to Hugo today. What's a realistic time for me to ring you back, tomorrow or is it better later in the week?"
Never end a call without a time. Getting them to agree when you ring back is what turns pestering into an appointment, and it puts a small deadline on them to have spoken to the vendor. Put the time and their name in the Houses tab. The money in this job is in the follow-up: most of these houses will still be sitting there in five weeks, and the vendor will be a lot keener then than they are today.
YOU: "That's great, thanks for your time [their name]. Speak to you then."
Then wait. Do not hang up on your own closing line. They often add the most useful thing in the call after they think it is over.
"Shall I book you in for a viewing?" obj
YOU: "Let me check Hugo's diary and come back to you, he'd want to be there. What have you got free later in the week?"
Take the availability, book nothing. Then mark it in the Houses tab so the director can pick it up.

Give your name and the number you are calling from. Nothing else without asking the director first.
